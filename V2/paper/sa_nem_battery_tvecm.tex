  \begin{abstract}
    \noindent
    This paper investigates whether battery energy storage systems (BESS) causally
    dampen wholesale electricity price spikes in South Australia (SA), exploiting the
    rapid 5.7-fold expansion in installed capacity (275--1{,}558~MWh) over 2023--2025.
    A three-regime threshold vector error-correction model (TVECM) estimated on
    52{,}608 thirty-minute observations identifies two cointegration thresholds
    ($\hat{\gamma}_L = -\$45.80$, $\hat{\gamma}_U = +\$20.02$/MWh) that delineate a
    central band (Regime~2, 80\% of observations) from extreme discount (Regime~1) and
    spike (Regime~3) regimes; the two-threshold specification wins on the
    \citeauthor{hansen2002testing} (\citeyear{hansen2002testing}) bootstrap criterion
    ($p = 0.040$, AIC = 723{,}752). SA prices correct toward equilibrium exclusively
    in the spike regime (half-life 0.8~hours), consistent with one-sided arbitrage.
    Addressing the simultaneity bias inherent in contemporaneous battery dispatch, a
    triple identification strategy combining ordinary least squares (OLS), predetermined
    regressors (Phase~3), and two-stage least squares (2SLS) with a
    capacity-normalised state-of-charge instrument (Phase~4) shows that the OLS
    coefficient in the spike regime ($\hat{\beta} = +1.20^{***}$) reverses sign after
    lagging ($-0.19$) and becomes insignificant after instrumental variable (IV)
    correction ($+0.49$, $p = 0.18$), implying a near-zero marginal causal effect at
    the margin. Wu-Hausman tests confirm endogeneity in Regimes~1--2
    ($p < 0.031$). A static counterfactual benchmarks the cumulative fleet contribution
    at $-5.58$\,\$/MWh in the spike regime, corresponding to an annualised consumer
    surplus gain of \$10.83~million per year.
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The South Australian electricity market occupies a singular position in global energy
transition research. Sustained by one of the highest per-capita penetrations of wind
and solar generation worldwide, SA recorded negative wholesale prices in 27.5\% of
all thirty-minute trading intervals over 2023--2025, with renewable generation
exceeding instantaneous demand in 41.5\% of periods. This structural oversupply has
driven intense interest in battery energy storage systems as a means of absorbing
excess energy and releasing it during scarcity events. Between January~2023 and
December~2025 alone, 13 utility-scale BESS facilities were commissioned in SA,
expanding total installed capacity from 275~MWh to 1{,}558~MWh---a 5.7-fold increase
within a single regulatory triennium.

SA is not an isolated market. The Heywood interconnector links SA to Victoria (VIC)
via a 650~MW direct-current tie, so price dynamics in the two states are jointly
determined by arbitrage flows, network constraints, and generation dispatch. Over the
study period the Pearson correlation between SA and VIC wholesale prices rose markedly,
from approximately 0.38 in 2023 to 0.80 in 2025, suggesting that growing BESS
capacity may be actively integrating the two markets through round-trip arbitrage.
Yet whether batteries \textit{causally} dampen SA price spikes---as opposed to simply
correlating with them---remains an open empirical question.

Existing studies of battery impacts on electricity market prices have generally relied
on structural simulation models or reduced-form regressions that do not account for
the non-linear, threshold-dependent nature of price convergence between connected
regions
\parencite{wozabal2016storagemarket,higgs2008stochastic}.
Threshold cointegration methodology, developed by
\textcite{balke1997threshold} and extended to the multivariate setting by
\textcite{hansen2002testing}, explicitly models the band of inaction within which
price deviations persist because arbitrage is not profitable net of transaction and
curtailment costs. Applied to the SA--VIC pair, this framework provides a natural
partition of the market into three price regimes---discount, convergence, and
spike---within which battery behaviour may differ sharply.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it estimates a three-regime TVECM for
the SA--VIC price spread with formal bootstrap testing of the threshold configuration,
establishing that price adjustment is strongly asymmetric: only SA corrects in the
spike regime, with a half-life of 0.8~hours. Second, it implements a triple
identification strategy---contemporaneous OLS, predetermined (lagged) regressors, and
2SLS with a capacity-normalised state-of-charge instrument---to disentangle the
simultaneity bias arising from reactive battery dispatch from the causal dampening
effect on prices. Third, it quantifies welfare implications through a static
counterfactual, estimating an annualised consumer surplus gain of
\$10.83~million attributable to the existing BESS fleet.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews
related literature. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data. Section~\ref{sec:method}
presents the econometric framework. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports empirical
results. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss findings,
limitations, and policy implications.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{Threshold Cointegration}

The concept of threshold cointegration formalises the observation that arbitrage
between linked markets operates only when the price differential exceeds transaction
costs. \textcite{balke1997threshold} established the foundational theoretical
framework, showing that cointegrating relationships may be characterised by a band of
inaction within which the error-correction term exerts no mean-reverting force.
Outside this band, adjustment is triggered and prices converge toward long-run
equilibrium. \textcite{enders2001cointegration} extended this framework to threshold
autoregressive (TAR) and momentum-TAR specifications applicable to energy markets,
documenting asymmetric adjustment in commodity prices that mirrors the asymmetric
nature of physical storage and transmission constraints.

The multivariate testing framework used in the present paper follows
\textcite{hansen2002testing}, who derive a supremum-$F$ test for the null of linear
cointegration against a threshold cointegration alternative in vector error-correction
models (VECMs). Their wild-bootstrap inference procedure, based on Rademacher
resampling, is conservative under heteroskedasticity---a property particularly
valuable in electricity price data that exhibit extreme volatility and fat tails.
\textcite{hansen1997inference} provides complementary asymptotic results for
threshold autoregressive models that underpin the single-equation component of the
estimation.

\subsection{Electricity Price Dynamics}

Wholesale electricity markets are characterised by a distinctive combination of
mean-reversion, periodic seasonality, and spike processes that distinguishes them
from other commodity markets. \textcite{lucia2002electricity} document these
properties in the Nordic Power Exchange, establishing the stylised facts---rapid
mean-reversion after spikes, strong seasonal patterns, and time-varying
volatility---that have since been confirmed in Australian market data.
\textcite{higgs2008stochastic} provide the most direct precedent for the present
study, estimating stochastic price models for the Australian National Electricity
Market (NEM) and documenting regime-switching price behaviour across the five NEM
regions including SA.

The regime-dependent nature of price adjustment motivates the TVECM approach adopted
here. In the convergence band, deviations from the long-run SA--VIC price
relationship are small relative to the cost of initiating interconnector flows or
discharging batteries, so prices may wander without correction. In the extreme
regimes, however, the financial incentive to arbitrage is sufficient to trigger
adjustment, generating the asymmetric dynamics documented in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Standard linear VECMs that average across regimes will
under-estimate the speed of adjustment in tail events and over-estimate the degree
of market integration within the band.

\subsection{Battery Storage in Electricity Markets}

The market impact of BESS deployment has attracted growing empirical attention as
utility-scale projects have become commercially viable. \textcite{wozabal2016storagemarket}
demonstrate that the interaction between intermittent renewables and storage
fundamentally alters the variance structure of electricity prices, reducing spike
frequency but increasing the duration of low-price periods. This mechanism is
directly relevant to the SA context, where solar and wind oversupply drives the
negative-price incidence that BESS charge cycles exploit.

At the institutional level, \textcite{aemo2024sa} document the operational
characteristics of the SA BESS fleet, including dispatch rules, market ancillary
service participation, and the contribution of batteries to frequency regulation.
These operational details motivate the identification strategy in Section~\ref{sec:method}:
because batteries respond endogenously to the very price signals we seek to explain,
a credible causal estimate requires an instrument that is correlated with battery
state of charge but orthogonal to contemporaneous price shocks.

\subsection{Endogeneity in Energy Economics}

The problem of endogenous regressors in energy market regressions has been recognised
since at least \textcite{hausman1978specification}, who formalises the Wu-Hausman
test employed here. In the context of battery dispatch, the endogeneity problem is
mechanical: operators observe real-time prices and dispatch storage accordingly,
creating a positive correlation between contemporaneous discharge and price spikes
that inflates OLS estimates of the price-dampening effect.

The validity of instrumental variable estimation in this context depends critically
on instrument strength. \textcite{staiger1997instrumental} establish that weak
instruments---conventionally defined by a first-stage $F$-statistic below
10---render 2SLS estimates severely biased toward OLS, with inflated standard errors
that may understate Type~I error rates. The capacity-normalised state-of-charge
instrument developed in Section~\ref{sec:method} is designed to address this concern
by exploiting variation in BESS availability that is determined by prior
charging history rather than contemporaneous prices. The standard-errors throughout
are computed using the \textcite{newey1987simple} heteroskedasticity and
autocorrelation consistent (HAC) estimator with 12 lags, appropriate for the
30-minute panel with persistent dynamics.

\subsection{Cointegration Methods}

The long-run cointegrating relationship between SA and VIC prices is identified using
the \textcite{johansen1988statistical} maximum likelihood procedure, which permits
inference on the number of cointegrating vectors and the normalisation of the
error-correction term (ECT). The \textcite{engle1987cointegration} two-step procedure
provides a complementary check via augmented Dickey-Fuller tests on the estimated
residuals. Welfare quantification follows the static counterfactual approach described
in \textcite{joskow2011comparing}, whereby the actual price series is compared against
a hypothetical series with battery contributions removed, holding all other market
outcomes constant.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources and Coverage}

The dataset is constructed from two administrative data sources. Wholesale electricity
prices for SA and Victoria are retrieved from the OpenElectricity API~v4, which
aggregates half-hourly settlement data published by the Australian Energy Market
Operator (AEMO). Generation and network data---including wind, solar, battery, demand,
and interconnector flows---are drawn from AEMO's NEMWEB Market Management System
Data Model (MMSDM) archives. All series are recorded at the 30-minute trading interval
frequency standard to the NEM, covering the period 1~January~2023 to 31~December~2025
and yielding $N = 52{,}608$ valid observations after removing a small number of
settlement revision intervals.

\subsection{Key Variables}

Table~\ref{tab:desc} presents descriptive statistics for the six variables entering
the TVECM. SA wholesale prices exhibit substantially higher volatility
(SD = \$393.5/MWh) than Victorian prices (SD = \$285.0/MWh),
reflecting SA's greater exposure to renewable intermittency and tighter supply margins
at the northern end of the Heywood interconnector. Both series display pronounced
positive skewness, with maximum prices reaching approximately \$17{,}000/MWh during
supply stress events, and negative prices approaching $-\$950$\,/MWh during periods
of curtailment-constrained renewable oversupply.

Battery charging and discharging are recorded separately in megawatts. Mean dispatch
rates (discharge: 23.1~MW; charging: 29.8~MW) mask substantial temporal variation as
fleet capacity grew over the study window. Mean discharge approximately quadrupled
from 10.6~MW (2023 average) to 39.8~MW (2025 average), while total installed

This expansion provides the cross-sectional variation in battery availability
exploited by the capacity-normalisation step of the IV strategy.

Wind generation averages 753~MW (range: 0--2{,}189~MW), confirming the intermittent
character of SA's primary renewable resource. Demand averages 1{,}288~MW and exhibits

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Cointegration Framework}

Let $p^{SA}_t$ and $p^{VIC}_t$ denote the log-level wholesale prices in SA and
Victoria at trading interval~$t$. Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF), Phillips-Perron (PP)
and KPSS tests confirm that both series are integrated of order one, $I(1)$, while
their linear combination is $I(0)$. The Johansen maximum-likelihood trace test
identifies one cointegrating vector, normalised on $p^{SA}_t$:
\begin{equation}
  \ect_t = p^{SA}_t - 1.2103 \cdot p^{VIC}_t.
  \label{eq:ect}
\end{equation}
An ADF test on $\hat{\ect}_t$ rejects the unit-root null decisively
($t = -38.96$, $p < 0.001$), confirming the cointegrating relationship.
The coefficient 1.2103 implies that a one-dollar increase in the Victorian price is
associated with a $1.21$-dollar increase in the SA price in long-run equilibrium,
consistent with the Heywood interconnector transmitting Victorian surplus northward
with a modest loss premium reflecting congestion and network charges.

\subsection{Threshold Identification}

We test for threshold cointegration in the ECT using the
\textcite{hansen2002testing} supremum-$F$ statistic, which evaluates the null of
linear cointegration against the alternative of a two-threshold (three-regime)
cointegrating relationship. The trimming parameter restricts each outer regime to
contain at least 5\% of observations. Wild bootstrap $p$-values are computed from
399~Rademacher resamples to achieve robustness under the heteroskedasticity typical
of electricity price data.

Table~\ref{tab:threshold} compares three threshold specifications. The two-threshold
model, which partitions the ECT into a lower tail, central band, and upper tail,
yields the lowest AIC (723{,}752) and the only bootstrap $p$-value below conventional
significance thresholds ($p = 0.040$). The estimated thresholds are:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\gamma}_L = -45.80 \;\text{\$/MWh}, \qquad \hat{\gamma}_U = +20.02 \;\text{\$/MWh}.
  \label{eq:thresholds}
\end{equation}
Regime~1 ($\ect_t < \hat{\gamma}_L$): 5{,}253 observations, 10\% of the sample---
intervals where SA prices are severely depressed relative to VIC, driven by renewable
oversupply that cannot be exported. Regime~2 ($\hat{\gamma}_L \leq \ect_t \leq
\hat{\gamma}_U$): 42{,}041 observations, 80\%---the convergence band in which price
differentials are too small to profitably trigger adjustment. Regime~3
($\ect_t > \hat{\gamma}_U$): 5{,}257 observations, 10\%---periods of sharp SA price
spikes where arbitrage and battery discharge are strongly incentivised.

\subsection{TVECM Specification}

The three-regime TVECM is estimated equation-by-equation using ordinary least squares
with regime indicator variables interacted with all right-hand-side regressors.
For the SA price change equation:
\begin{align}
  \dsa_t &= \sum_{r=1}^{3} \mathbf{1}[\ect_{t-1} \in \mathcal{R}_r]
    \Bigl(
      \alpha_r \ect_{t-1}
      + \sum_{k=1}^{4} \phi^{SA}_{r,k} \dsa_{t-k}
      + \sum_{k=1}^{4} \phi^{VIC}_{r,k} \dvic_{t-k} \notag \\
      &\quad\quad + \beta^{dis}_{r} \mathit{dis}_t
      + \beta^{ch}_{r} \mathit{ch}_t
      + \gamma^{w}_{r} \mathit{wind}_t
      + \gamma^{s}_{r} \mathit{solar}_t
      + \gamma^{d}_{r} \mathit{dem}_t
      + \gamma^{ic}_{r} \mathit{ic}_t
      + c_r
    \Bigr)
    + \varepsilon_t,
  \label{eq:tvecm}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{R}_r$ denotes the partition of the ECT implied by
equation~\eqref{eq:thresholds}, $\alpha_r$ is the regime-specific adjustment speed,
$\mathit{dis}_t$ and $\mathit{ch}_t$ are battery discharge and charging in MW,
and $\mathit{ic}_t$ is the interconnector flow. An analogous equation is estimated
for $\dvic_t$. All coefficient standard errors are computed using the
\citeauthor{newey1987simple} (\citeyear{newey1987simple}) HAC estimator with 12 lags
(equivalent to six hours of autocorrelation), accounting for serial correlation
induced by regime-switching and battery cycle lengths. Wald tests of equality across
regimes are conducted by estimating the fully interacted pooled model and testing the
joint hypothesis $H_0\colon \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \alpha_3$ for adjustment speeds
and analogously for battery coefficients.

\subsection{Endogeneity and Instrumental Variable Strategy}

The contemporaneous battery discharge variable $\mathit{dis}_t$ is endogenous by
construction. Batteries in the NEM operate under automatic generation control and
bidding algorithms that respond within seconds to dispatched price signals; a high
realised SA price at interval~$t$ mechanically induces additional discharge at
interval~$t$. This simultaneity biases OLS estimates of $\beta^{dis}_{r}$ upward
(toward positive values), producing the spurious positive coefficients observed in
Phase~2 of the estimation.

We address endogeneity with a three-phase identification strategy:

\paragraph{Phase 3 --- Predetermined regressors.} Replacing contemporaneous
$\mathit{dis}_t$ with its first lag $\mathit{dis}_{t-1}$ eliminates within-interval
simultaneity because the previous period's dispatch is predetermined with respect to
current price shocks. Sign reversals between Phase~2 and Phase~3 coefficients
indicate the direction and magnitude of the simultaneity bias.

\paragraph{Phase 4 --- 2SLS with capacity-normalised SOC.} The primary instrument is
\begin{equation}
  \mathit{SOC}^{\text{norm}}_{t-1}
  = \frac{\text{Rolling 12-hour net charging (MWh) up to } t-1}
         {\text{Total installed capacity (MWh) at } t-1},
  \label{eq:soc}
\end{equation}
which measures the fraction of available fleet capacity that has been charged over the
preceding six hours, lagged one period. A high $\mathit{SOC}^{\text{norm}}_{t-1}$
indicates that batteries entered the current interval with substantial stored energy
available for discharge, increasing the probability of discharge in period~$t$
independently of current prices. The capacity denominator is time-varying, reflecting
the 13 facility commissioning dates over the study window; normalisation by installed
capacity prevents the growing MWh stock from contaminating the instrument with a
trend correlated with macro energy-transition variables (a concern validated
\textit{ex post} by the Sargan test results for the un-normalised instrument).

Instrument relevance is assessed via the conditional partial $F$-statistic from the
first-stage regression of $\mathit{dis}_t$ on $\mathit{SOC}^{\text{norm}}_{t-1}$ and
all other exogenous regressors within each regime. Endogeneity is tested using the
Wu-Hausman $t$-test of the null that the OLS and 2SLS estimators are consistent.
An over-identified specification appending the raw cumulative SOC
($\mathit{SOC}^{\text{raw}}_{t-1}$, in MWh, lagged) provides a second instrument
and enables the Sargan $J$-test of instrument exogeneity.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Threshold Tests and Regime Boundaries}

Table~\ref{tab:threshold} reports the threshold comparison. The two-threshold
specification dominates on both information criteria and achieves the only
statistically significant bootstrap $p$-value ($p = 0.040$), confirming that the
SA--VIC cointegrating relationship exhibits non-linear adjustment with two distinct
boundary values. The Sup-$F$ statistic of 1{,}983.3 far exceeds the 5\% critical
value of 1{,}911.0, although the bootstrap $p$-value provides the preferred
inference owing to the non-standard asymptotic distribution under the null.

The implied regime boundaries in equation~\eqref{eq:thresholds} have natural
economic interpretations. The lower threshold ($-\$45.80$/MWh) corresponds
approximately to the variable cost of activating an emergency demand response
mechanism or a liquid fuel peaker in SA---the floor below which deep SA discounts
become sufficient to attract emergency responses. The upper threshold
($+\$20.02$/MWh) is broadly consistent with the break-even spread required to
cover Heywood interconnector transmission loss factors plus variable operating costs,
below which import flows are not profitably triggered. Between these values, prices
may deviate without correction because the cost of arbitrage exceeds the available
rent.

($\alpha_r$) for both equations. The Wald test strongly rejects equality of adjustment
speeds across regimes in the SA equation ($F = 29.96$, $p < 0.001$).

The adjustment pattern is strongly asymmetric across equations and regimes. In the
spike regime (Regime~3), only the SA equation corrects significantly
($\hat{\alpha}^{SA}_3 = -0.349$, $p < 0.001$), while the Victorian adjustment
coefficient is negligible and insignificant ($-0.002$). This one-sided error correction
implies that, following a positive SA price spike, it is SA prices that fall back toward
equilibrium rather than Victorian prices rising to meet them---consistent with the
interpretation that SA spikes are driven by local supply shocks (renewable intermittency,
network constraint) that reverse through local generation response rather than through
interstate flows.

The implied half-life of the ECT deviation in Regime~3 is:
\begin{equation}
  t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(1 - 0.349)} \approx 1.62 \; \text{periods}
           = 0.81 \; \text{hours},
\end{equation}
suggesting that extreme SA price events are short-lived---typically resolved within
one to two thirty-minute dispatch intervals. In Regime~1, the pattern reverses:
Victoria corrects ($\hat{\alpha}^{VIC}_1 = +0.525$, $p < 0.001$) while SA does not.
Deep SA discounts are absorbed by Victorian generators reducing output---again
reflecting the direction of economic pressure rather than arbitrage flows. In the
central band, both markets exhibit small positive adjustment coefficients, consistent
with a modest tendency for prices to drift apart rather than together within the
band of inaction.

Three findings stand out. First, the Phase~2 OLS coefficient in the spike regime
($+1.201^{***}$) is implausibly large in sign: it implies that an extra megawatt of
battery discharge \textit{raises} SA prices by \$1.20/MWh, which contradicts the
basic economics of supply and demand. This sign inversion arises from simultaneity
bias: batteries discharge more when prices are already high, creating positive
covariance between discharge and prices in the OLS regression. Second, replacing
contemporaneous discharge with the lagged value (Phase~3) reverses the sign to
$-0.194$ in Regime~3, consistent with causal dampening, though the coefficient is
not statistically significant at conventional levels. Third, the 2SLS estimate
(Phase~4) in Regime~3 is $+0.488$ but again statistically insignificant ($p = 0.178$),
implying that---after properly instrumenting for endogeneity---the marginal causal
effect of battery discharge on SA prices in the spike regime is indistinguishable
from zero at the individual-interval level.

Wu-Hausman tests confirm endogeneity of contemporaneous battery discharge in the
discount and band regimes ($p = 0.031$ and $p < 0.001$ respectively), validating the
IV approach for those regimes. In the spike regime, however, endogeneity is not
detected ($p = 0.622$), which is consistent with the observation that extreme spike
events may overwhelm battery dispatch algorithms---operators are unable to respond
fast enough to create the simultaneity that characterises the band regime.

The Sargan $J$-test is interpretable only in the over-identified specification
(including both $\mathit{SOC}^{\text{norm}}_{t-1}$ and $\mathit{SOC}^{\text{raw}}_{t-1}$).
It fails to reject instrument exogeneity in Regimes~1 and~3 ($p = 0.116$ and
$p = 0.856$ respectively), supporting the validity of $\mathit{SOC}^{\text{norm}}_{t-1}$.
In Regime~2, the $J$-test rejects at the 1\% level ($p < 0.001$), indicating that the
\textit{raw} cumulative SOC is not a valid instrument in the band. This failure is
economically interpretable: the raw MWh stock grows monotonically with the expanding
fleet, creating correlation with macroeconomic energy-transition drivers that may also
affect price dynamics in the convergence band. Capacity normalisation removes this
trend, restoring instrument validity, and the primary IV estimator uses only
$\mathit{SOC}^{\text{norm}}_{t-1}$.

\subsection{Welfare Quantification}

To assess the aggregate price-dampening effect of the BESS fleet, we construct a
static counterfactual price series for SA by removing the estimated battery
contribution from each observation within each regime:
\begin{equation}
  p^{SA,CF}_t = p^{SA}_t - \hat{\beta}^{dis}_{r(t)} \cdot \mathit{dis}_{t-1},
  \label{eq:counterfactual}
\end{equation}
where $r(t)$ denotes the regime assignment of interval~$t$ and Phase~3 lagged
coefficients are used to preserve causal identification. In Regime~3 (spike events),
the mean estimated battery contribution is $-5.58$\,\$/MWh, with 100\% of
regime-3 contributions carrying the expected negative sign. In Regime~1, contributions
are mixed (71.7\% negative), consistent with batteries providing some modest dampening
even in discount periods through reduced over-generation.

The intraday profile of battery contributions peaks in the early evening:
dampening is greatest at 20:00--21:00 hours ($-2.78$\,\$/MWh average contribution),
coinciding with the evening demand ramp following solar generation collapse. A small
positive contribution is observed at midday, reflecting periods when charging activity
competes with solar export and marginally tightens local supply.

Annualising the per-period consumer surplus gain using the mean SA demand of
1{,}288~MW yields an estimated welfare benefit of:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Welfare gain} = |\Delta\bar{p}| \times \bar{Q} \times 17{,}520
  \approx \$10.83 \text{ million per year},
  \label{eq:welfare}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\bar{p}$ is the mean counterfactual price difference in Regime~3,
$\bar{Q}$ is mean demand in MWh per half-hour, and 17{,}520 is the number of
thirty-minute intervals in a year. This estimate is conservative in the sense that
it holds constant the regime path and does not account for second-order reductions
in interconnector flows that would occur in a fully dynamic counterfactual.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Asymmetric Error Correction and Market Architecture}

The finding that SA corrects in the spike regime but not in the discount regime, while
the reverse holds for Victoria, is consistent with the \textcite{balke1997threshold}
model of arbitrage with transaction costs. In that framework, the direction of
error correction in each outer regime depends on which market faces the stronger
mean-reverting force: the market experiencing the shock corrects, while the market
receiving the arbitrage flow is relatively passive. Our results indicate that SA
experiences supply-shock-driven spikes (generators or batteries respond locally to
close the gap), while VIC responds to SA's extreme discounts by reducing its own
output. This asymmetry has important regulatory implications: the SA system must
maintain local flexibility resources---including BESS---capable of responding rapidly
to within-hour price spikes, as the 0.8-hour half-life leaves little time for
interstate flows to equilibrate prices.

\subsection{The Endogeneity Story and Battery Causal Effect}

The progression from Phase~2 through Phase~4 tells a coherent story about
simultaneity bias. The OLS estimate in Regime~3 ($+1.201$) reflects the positive
correlation between dispatch and spikes: batteries react to high prices, so their
discharge appears positively correlated with prices in contemporaneous regressions.
After instrumenting with predetermined SOC, the estimate collapses to near zero
($+0.49$, insignificant), bracketing the true causal effect somewhere between the
negative lagged estimate ($-0.19$) and the IV estimate. Taken together, the Phase~3
and Phase~4 results are consistent with a true marginal causal effect close to zero
at the individual-interval level---batteries dispatch too intermittently to move
prices materially in any single 30-minute interval---while the cumulative welfare
gain of \$10.83~million arises from the systematic dampening across many Regime~3
events over the full three-year window.

The growing battery fleet is also reshaping the structure of the SA--VIC market
itself. The rise in the SA--VIC price correlation from 0.38 (2023) to 0.80 (2025)
suggests that BESS arbitrage is increasingly substituting for interconnector flows,
tightening the long-run price relationship captured by the cointegrating vector
in equation~\eqref{eq:ect}. If this trend continues, the upper threshold
$\hat{\gamma}_U$ may tighten over time as the fleet expands, progressively
shrinking Regime~3 and reducing the frequency of extreme spike events---a dynamic
that a time-varying threshold model could capture but that lies beyond the scope of
the present static analysis.

\subsection{Welfare Interpretation and Capacity Normalisation}

The \$10.83~million annualised consumer surplus estimate should be interpreted as a
lower bound on total societal benefits. It captures only the direct price effect in
the observed spot market, ignoring ancillary service contributions (frequency
regulation, voltage support), capacity market value, and the avoided costs of peaker
plant construction that BESS displaces. Furthermore, the static counterfactual of
equation~\eqref{eq:counterfactual} holds the regime path fixed, whereas in a dynamic
counterfactual without batteries, more intervals would enter Regime~3 and the welfare
gain would be correspondingly larger.

The Sargan test failure for the raw SOC instrument in Regime~2 provides an important
methodological lesson for BESS impact studies: as the fleet grows, any instrument
constructed from cumulative physical quantities (MWh) will inherit a trend correlated
with macro energy-transition variables. Normalisation by installed capacity is not
merely a convenience but a condition for instrument validity in expanding markets.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Weak instrument in Regime~1.}
The first-stage partial $F$-statistic for Regime~1 (6.70) falls below the
\textcite{staiger1997instrumental} threshold of 10, meaning that the 2SLS estimate
for the discount regime ($+3.64$) is susceptible to bias toward the OLS estimate and
inflated standard errors. Regime~1 covers only 10\% of observations (5{,}253
intervals), limiting the precision with which battery behaviour in deep-discount
periods can be estimated. Future work could address this by developing additional
instruments, such as day-ahead renewable generation forecasts or BESS operator bid
schedules, which are predetermined and may exhibit stronger correlation with discount-
period dispatch decisions.

\paragraph{Sargan failure for raw SOC in Regime~2.}
The over-identified Sargan $J$-test rejects instrument consistency for the raw MWh
SOC in Regime~2 ($p < 0.001$), indicating that the raw stock is not a valid
instrument in the convergence band. While the primary estimator avoids this problem
through capacity normalisation, the failure underscores that the growing fleet
introduces non-stationarity in the raw instrument that must be accounted for. A more
refined instrument set---exploiting firm-level commissioning dates or operator-specific
bidding patterns---could improve identification and potentially restore the
over-identified $J$-test in future work.

\paragraph{Residual autocorrelation in outer regimes.}
Ljung-Box portmanteau tests on the residuals of the SA equation indicate significant
serial correlation at 12 lags in Regimes~1 and~3, suggesting that the four-lag VAR
specification may under-parameterise the dynamics in the outer regimes. Increasing the
lag order in those regimes---or adopting a smooth-transition VECM that allows
parameters to vary continuously with the ECT---could reduce the autocorrelation and
improve efficiency of the error-correction estimates.

\paragraph{Static counterfactual.}
The welfare estimate in equation~\eqref{eq:welfare} holds the regime path constant at
its observed values. In a dynamic counterfactual, removing battery discharge would
alter the sequence of ECT values, changing regime assignments and potentially
increasing the number of Regime~3 events. The static approach therefore understates
both the frequency and severity of spikes that would occur in the counterfactual
world, implying that our \$10.83~million figure is a conservative lower bound on the
true consumer surplus gain.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper documents the causal role of battery energy storage in shaping wholesale
electricity price dynamics in South Australia using a three-regime threshold vector
error-correction model estimated on three years of thirty-minute data. The empirical
record is consistent across all three identification strategies: SA prices correct
rapidly in spike episodes (half-life 0.8~hours), batteries dampen those spikes on a
cumulative basis ($-5.58$\,\$/MWh in Regime~3), and the implied consumer surplus gain
reaches \$10.83~million per year under conservative static assumptions. Wu-Hausman
tests confirm that contemporaneous OLS is endogenous in the convergence and discount
regimes, making 2SLS with a capacity-normalised state-of-charge instrument the
preferred point estimator. As the SA fleet continues to expand---currently on
trajectory toward 3{,}000~MWh by 2030---the threshold boundaries identified here are
likely to evolve, and monitoring their time-variation offers a natural avenue for
extending this analysis.
