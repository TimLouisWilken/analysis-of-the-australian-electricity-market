  \begin{abstract}
    \noindent
    This paper investigates whether battery energy storage systems (BESS)
    causally dampen wholesale electricity price spikes in South Australia (SA),
    exploiting the rapid 4.6-fold expansion in installed capacity
    (285--1{,}296~MWh) over 2023--2025.  A three-regime threshold vector
    error-correction model (TVECM) estimated on 52{,}608 thirty-minute
    observations identifies two cointegration thresholds
    ($\hat{\gamma}_{L} = -\$45.80$, $\hat{\gamma}_{U} = +\$20.02$/MWh,
    Hansen--Seo bootstrap $p = 0.040$) that delineate a no-arbitrage band from
    extreme discount and spike regimes.  SA prices correct toward equilibrium
    exclusively in the spike regime (half-life 0.8~hours).  A triple
    identification strategy---contemporaneous OLS, predetermined regressors,
    and two-stage least squares (2SLS) with a capacity-normalised
    state-of-charge instrument validated by a novel commissioning-event
    instrument ($Z^{newcap}$, Sargan $p = 0.174$)---shows that the OLS
    battery-discharge coefficient in the spike regime ($+1.20^{***}$) reverses
    sign under predetermined regressors ($-0.19$) and becomes insignificant
    under IV correction, implying a near-zero marginal causal effect.
    A TVECM-GARCH-M extension confirms full coefficient robustness under
    IGARCH dynamics and heavy tails ($\nu \approx 2.9$).  Capacity-interaction
    estimates demonstrate that the OLS simultaneity bias declined by 94\%
    in the spike regime and passed through zero in the band regime by
    end-of-sample (December 2025), consistent with fleet maturation from
    reactive to strategic dispatch.  A conservative static scenario estimate places
    the annualised consumer surplus gain at \$10.83~million---a figure
    whose aggregate validity is corroborated by the macro-level decline
    in spike-regime frequency from 9.4\% (2023) to 5.0\% (2025).
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

South Australia occupies a unique position in global energy-transition research.
With wind and solar generation averaging approximately 90\% of operational demand
over the 2023--2025 period studied here, the state recorded negative wholesale
prices in 27.5\% of all thirty-minute trading intervals, and renewable supply
exceeded instantaneous demand in 41.5\% of periods.  This structural oversupply
coexists with a long right tail of extreme price spikes: 0.4\% of intervals
recorded prices above \$1{,}000/MWh, with a maximum exceeding \$16{,}970/MWh.
Against this backdrop the SA battery energy storage system (BESS) fleet expanded
4.6-fold in three years, from approximately 285~MWh (January 2023) to 1{,}296~MWh
(December 2025), driven by the commissioning of the 250~MWh Torrens Island
facility (August 2023), the 477~MWh Blyth project (April 2025), and others.

SA is not an isolated market.  The Heywood interconnector links SA to Victoria
(VIC) via a 650~MW tie, so wholesale prices in both states are jointly determined
by arbitrage flows, generation dispatch, and network constraints.  Over the study
period the Pearson correlation between SA and VIC prices rose sharply, from 0.38
(2023) to 0.80 (2025), suggesting that growing battery capacity actively
integrates the two markets through round-trip arbitrage.  Yet whether batteries
\textit{causally} dampen SA price spikes---as opposed to merely correlating with
them---remains an open empirical question.

The central identification challenge is a simultaneity paradox: batteries respond
to price spikes by discharging, generating a positive empirical correlation
between discharge volumes and prices.  A naïve OLS regression accordingly yields
$\hat{\beta}_{dis} = +1.20^{***}$ in the spike regime, suggesting
battery dispatch raises prices---the opposite of the theoretical prediction.
Disentangling this simultaneity bias demands either predetermined regressors or
credible instrumental variables.  At the same time, existing cross-regional
price-integration studies, such as \textcite{stanciu2025price}'s VECM analysis
of 24 EU electricity markets, implicitly assume linear and symmetric price
adjustment, whereas threshold dynamics governed by transmission costs create
regime-specific arbitrage corridors that a linear model cannot identify.

This paper makes three contributions.  First, it estimates a three-regime TVECM
for the SA--VIC price pair, demonstrating that error-correction is strongly
asymmetric: SA alone corrects in the spike regime (half-life 0.8~hours), while
VIC alone adjusts in the discount regime.  Second, it implements a triple
identification strategy that resolves the simultaneity paradox, employing a
commissioning-event instrument ($Z^{newcap}$) whose exogeneity rests on
construction-contract timelines rather than market signals; this instrument
passes the Sargan over-identification test in all three regimes, providing clean
external validation.  Third, it extends the TVECM with a GARCH-in-mean
specification, confirming that the battery-discharge coefficients are fully
robust to IGARCH dynamics and extreme-tail heteroscedasticity.  Complementing
these structural findings, capacity-interaction estimates document a 94\%
decline in the OLS simultaneity-bias coefficient in the spike regime---and a
complete passage through zero in the band regime---from 2023 to December 2025,
consistent with a maturing BESS fleet transitioning from reactive to strategic dispatch.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsection*{Market integration and threshold dynamics}

The canonical framework for cross-regional price integration is the vector
error-correction model.  \textcite{stanciu2025price} apply a VECM to daily
wholesale prices across 24 EU electricity markets, identifying regional
integration clusters and dynamic short-term adjustment paths.  While their
analysis demonstrates the value of cointegration methods for electricity markets,
it assumes symmetric, linear adjustment.  This assumption is inappropriate when
arbitrage is feasible only once a price spread exceeds a corridor of transaction,
curtailment, and network costs---the ``band of inaction'' formalised by
\textcite{balke1997threshold}.  \textcite{hansen2002testing} extend this
framework to multivariate threshold cointegration, which is the identifying
structure exploited in the present paper.  The SA--VIC pair is a natural
candidate: the 650~MW Heywood interconnector imposes hard capacity constraints,
and renewable oversupply creates asymmetric correction dynamics that a symmetric
VECM would average away.

\subsection*{Time-varying price convergence}

\textcite{demenezes2016reassessing} reassess European electricity market
integration using fractional cointegration and find that price convergence is
heterogeneous and evolves over time as market-coupling infrastructure expands.
Their finding that integration parameters are non-stationary motivates two
components of the present analysis: the rolling-window threshold estimation
(Section~\ref{sec:results2}), which documents that the spike-regime frequency
declined from 9.4\% (2023) to 5.0\% (2025) as battery capacity grew; and the
capacity-interaction model, which tests formally whether the battery-price
relationship itself changes with fleet size.  \textcite{hauzenberger2023sparse}
propose sparse time-varying parameter VECMs as a flexible alternative, but their
framework does not readily accommodate regime-switching triggered by economic
thresholds; the TVECM approach adopted here offers sharper economic interpretability
for a market where the band-of-inaction mechanism is structurally motivated.

\subsection*{Renewable energy and price extremes}

\textcite{mwampashi2025beyond} examine the entire distribution of Australian
NEM spot prices using quantile regression, finding that variable renewable
energy effectively lowers prices at lower quantiles but has negligible impact at
upper quantiles during spike events, where gas generation remains the dominant
marginal setter.  Their analysis defines the precise regime in which the present
paper focuses: the upper tail of the price distribution, where BESS discharge
constitutes the fastest-responding alternative to gas peakers.  While
\textcite{mwampashi2025beyond} document the problem---renewable energy cannot
contain extreme spikes---this paper addresses the causal solution: whether
battery storage can.

\subsection*{Research gap}

No prior study applies a threshold-cointegrated, instrument-identified model to
the SA--VIC price pair to quantify the causal BESS effect on regime-conditional
price dynamics.  The combination of a novel commissioning-event instrument,
GARCH-in-mean robustness checking, and a capacity-interaction test for fleet
maturation constitutes the methodological contribution of this paper.

\section{Data and Market Context}
\label{sec:data}

The dataset comprises 52{,}608 thirty-minute settlement intervals from
2023-01-01 to 2025-12-31, drawn from the OpenElectricity API v4 for SA1 and
VIC1 regional reference prices, wind and solar generation, and operational
demand, and from the NEMWEB MMSDM database for interconnector flows and BESS
dispatch.  Prices are censored at $\pm$15{,}001\,\$/MWh to remove the five
market-cap/floor events.  Descriptive statistics are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:desc} (Appendix~A).

SA wholesale prices display an extreme bimodal distribution: a mean of
88.98\,\$/MWh masks a standard deviation of 393.52\,\$/MWh, with 27.5\% of
observations negative.  Battery discharge averages 23.1~MW but grew sharply
over the sample, from a mean of 10.6~MW in 2023 to 39.8~MW in 2025 (Table~\ref{tab:fleet}).
Twelve utility-scale BESS facilities were operational in SA by the end of 2025,
with total installed capacity rising from 285~MWh to 1{,}296~MWh (4.6-fold
growth).  Four in-sample commissioning events provide the exogenous capacity
variation exploited by the $Z^{newcap}$ instrument (Section~\ref{sec:method}):
Torrens Island (250~MWh, August 2023), Tailem Bend~2 (84~MWh, January 2025),
Blyth (477~MWh, April 2025), and Mannum (200~MWh, October 2025).

The sharp rise in SA--VIC price correlation, from 0.38 (2023) to 0.80 (2025),
is the macro signature of growing BESS-mediated market integration.  The
simultaneous compression of the mean spread from 25.3\,\$/MWh (2023) to
8.8\,\$/MWh (2025) supports the hypothesis that batteries are actively
arbitraging away persistent price divergences.  These time-varying patterns
motivate both the rolling-window threshold analysis and the capacity-interaction
model.

\section{Econometric Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Three-Regime TVECM}

Let $p_t^{SA}$ and $p_t^{VIC}$ denote SA and VIC log-spot prices.  Johansen
(1988) maximum-likelihood estimation of a four-lag VAR identifies one
cointegrating vector (Table~\ref{tab:johansen}), yielding the error-correction term
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ect}
  \ect_{t} = p_t^{SA} - \hat{\beta}\, p_t^{VIC}, \qquad \hat{\beta} = 1.2103.
\end{equation}
The ADF statistic on $\ect_t$ is $-38.96$ ($p < 0.001$), confirming stationarity.

Regime membership is determined by the lagged $\ect_{t-1}$ relative to two
thresholds $\gamma_L < \gamma_U$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:regime}
  r_t =
  \begin{cases}
    1 & \ect_{t-1} < \gamma_L \quad (\text{SA discount}) \\
    2 & \gamma_L \le \ect_{t-1} \le \gamma_U \quad (\text{no-arbitrage band}) \\
    3 & \ect_{t-1} > \gamma_U \quad (\text{SA price spike})
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The \textcite{hansen2002testing} two-threshold bootstrap test selects
$\hat\gamma_L = -45.80$\,\$/MWh and $\hat\gamma_U = +20.02$\,\$/MWh
($p = 0.040$, AIC = 723{,}752), decisively outperforming single-threshold and
symmetric-band alternatives (Table~\ref{tab:thresh}).  The three regimes contain 10\%
(Regime~1, $N = 5{,}253$), 80\% (Regime~2, $N = 42{,}042$), and 10\%
(Regime~3, $N = 5{,}257$) of observations respectively.

Within each regime $r \in \{1,2,3\}$, the TVECM is estimated by OLS with
Newey--West HAC standard errors (12 lags):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tvecm}
  \dsa_t = \mu_r + \alpha_r\,\ect_{t-1}
  + \sum_{i=1}^{4}\phi_i^r\,\dsa_{t-i}
  + \sum_{i=1}^{4}\psi_i^r\,\dvic_{t-i}
  + \beta_1^r B_t^{dis} + \beta_2^r B_t^{chg}
  + \boldsymbol{\delta}^{r\prime}\mathbf{x}_t + \varepsilon_t
\end{equation}
where $B_t^{dis}$ and $B_t^{chg}$ are battery discharge and charging (MW),
$\mathbf{x}_t$ collects wind, solar, demand, and interconnector flows, and the
identical equation is estimated for $\dvic_t$.  Lag order $L = 4$ is
AIC/BIC-optimal in Regime~2 (the dominant regime); lag robustness across
$L \in \{4,6,8,10,12\}$ confirms that key coefficients change by at most 0.04
(Table~\ref{tab:lagrobust}).

\subsection{Identification Strategy}

\textbf{The simultaneity problem.}  Battery operators observe real-time prices
and dispatch in response to them; a contemporaneous OLS regression therefore
conflates the causal dampening effect with the reactive dispatch channel,
producing an upward-biased $\hat\beta_1^r$.  Three identification strategies
address this.

\textbf{Phase~3: predetermined regressors.}  Replacing $B_t^{dis}$ with
$B_{t-1}^{dis}$ removes the contemporaneous simultaneity channel while
preserving regime heterogeneity.

\textbf{Phase~4: 2SLS with capacity-normalised SOC instrument.}  The primary
instrument is the capacity-normalised state of charge:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:soc}
  Z^{SOC}_{t-1} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{23}
    \bigl(B_{t-j}^{chg} - B_{t-j}^{dis}\bigr)\cdot 0.5\text{\,h}}{K_{t-1}}
\end{equation}
where $K_{t-1}$ is total installed BESS capacity (MWh) at time $t-1$, and the
numerator is the rolling 12-hour net energy accumulation.  High lagged SOC
implies more discharge headroom, creating a relevant first stage; capacity
normalisation eliminates the growing-fleet time trend that contaminates
unnormalised SOC (the Sargan J-test for raw MWh fails in Regime~2 at $p<0.001$).

The commissioning-event instrument $Z^{newcap}_{t-1}$ counts the total megawatt-hours
commissioned in the 14-day window ending at $t-1$.  Its exogeneity rests on
construction-contract timelines, which are determined years in advance by
project finance decisions---not by real-time market conditions.  The instrument
records approximately 2{,}681 non-zero observations concentrated around four
in-sample commissioning dates.  Wu-Hausman augmented regression tests
\parencite{hausman1978specification} and Sargan over-identification tests
\parencite{sargan1958estimation} validate both instruments jointly (Table~\ref{tab:ivdiag}).

\subsection{GARCH-in-Mean Extension}

Ljung-Box tests on TVECM residuals reveal significant autocorrelation in
Regimes~1 and 3 at all lag orders, which lag-robustness analysis confirms is
volatility clustering rather than mean-equation mis-specification
(Section~\ref{sec:results2}).  The GARCH-in-mean (GARCH-M) extension
\parencite{engle1982autoregressive} augments equation~\eqref{eq:tvecm} with the
conditional standard deviation:
\begin{align}
  \varepsilon_t &= \sigma_t\, z_t, \qquad z_t \sim t(\nu), \label{eq:garchm_mean}\\
  \sigma_t^2    &= \omega_r + \alpha_G\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2
                  + \beta_G\,\sigma_{t-1}^2, \label{eq:garchm_var}\\
  \dsa_t        &= \cdots + \delta_r\,\sigma_t + \varepsilon_t \label{eq:risk}
\end{align}
where $\delta_r$ captures any risk premium in the conditional mean.  Estimation
uses the iterated two-step feasible GARCH-M procedure of
\textcite{engle1982autoregressive}, converging in at most nine iterations per
regime.  The primary purpose of this extension is robustness verification: if
$\hat\beta_1^r$ changes materially after accounting for IGARCH dynamics, the
OLS identification strategy would be suspect.

\section{Results~I: Asymmetric Dynamics and Causal Battery Effects}
\label{sec:results1}

\subsection{Error-Correction Asymmetry}

Table~\ref{tab:alpha} reports the error-correction speeds $\alpha_r$ for the SA
and VIC equations.  The Wald test strongly rejects equality across regimes
($F(2) = 29.96$, $p < 0.001$), validating the three-regime TVECM against a
linear VECM.

In \textbf{Regime~3} (SA price spike), only the SA equation exhibits a
significant error-correction response: $\hat\alpha_{SA} = -0.349$
($p < 0.001$), implying that SA prices revert toward the cointegrating
relationship with a half-life of approximately 0.8~hours.  The VIC adjustment
speed is statistically indistinguishable from zero ($\hat\alpha_{VIC} = -0.002$,
$p = 0.14$).  The economic mechanism is directional: when SA prices spike above
the no-arbitrage ceiling, VIC imports via the Heywood interconnector suppress the
premium.  VIC prices are unaffected because the correction occurs through
inter-state flows, not VIC-side price formation.

In \textbf{Regime~1} (SA deep discount), the pattern reverses: VIC corrects
alone ($\hat\alpha_{VIC} = +0.525^{***}$), falling toward the level implied by
cheap SA renewable exports.  The SA adjustment is insignificant
($\hat\alpha_{SA} = +0.213$, $p = 0.18$), reflecting that SA generators already
operating at capacity cannot reduce output further.  This price-leadership
asymmetry is consistent with the band-of-inaction model of
\textcite{balke1997threshold}: correction occurs only from the side that
benefits from arbitrage, and only once the spread exceeds the transaction-cost
threshold.

\textbf{Impulse-response functions} (Figure~\ref{fig:irf}) make this asymmetry
visually transparent.  A 1~MW battery discharge shock dissipates almost
entirely within six 30-minute periods in Regime~3: the SA price level at
horizon $h = 12$ is only $+0.026$\,\$/MWh, compared with the initial period
impact of $+1.20$\,\$/MWh.  In Regime~1, the identical shock persists at
$+0.268$\,\$/MWh after six hours because the positive but insignificant
$\hat\alpha_{SA}$ generates no restoring force.  The VIC response in the spike
regime is negligible ($+0.010$\,\$/MWh at $h = 12$), confirming that the
battery-price channel is SA-local in the spike regime.  The IRF confidence bands
(80\%, wild Rademacher bootstrap, $N = 500$) bracket the point estimates tightly
in Regime~2 but widen substantially in the outer regimes, reflecting greater
coefficient uncertainty under small-sample spike conditions.

\subsection{Identification of the Causal Battery Effect}

Table~\ref{tab:batteries} presents $\hat\beta_1^r$ across the three
identification strategies.  The contrast is stark in the spike regime.
The contrast is most instructive in \textbf{Regime~3} (spike).
The contemporaneous OLS estimate ($+1.201^{***}$) is dominated by reactive
dispatch: batteries discharge precisely when prices spike, inflating the OLS
coefficient through positive simultaneity.  Replacing the contemporaneous
regressor with its one-period lag removes this channel and reverses the sign
($-0.194$, $n.s.$), consistent with a causal dampening effect that, while
economically meaningful in the aggregate, is statistically imprecise at the
individual period level.  The 2SLS estimate ($+0.755^{**}$) captures the
exogenously driven component of discharge.  Phase~3 and Phase~4 estimates
therefore bracket the true causal effect between $-0.19$ and $+0.76$,
consistent with near-zero at the margin.

The \textbf{Regime~1} IV estimate ($+1.754^{***}$) requires a frank
interpretation.  In the extreme-discount regime, battery \textit{discharge}
is sparse---mean discharge is only $\approx$8~MW in Regime~1 versus 63~MW in
Regime~3, because the dominant activity is charging into cheap renewable
surplus.  The strong positive 2SLS coefficient most plausibly captures the
transition moment when renewable oversupply begins to abate, the battery
switches from charging to discharging, and price recovers simultaneously:
discharge and price recovery are both driven by the same improving supply
condition.  This is a statistical artefact of low discharge variation in the
discount regime, not evidence of a causal price-raising mechanism, and it is
consistent with the lagged OLS estimate being effectively zero ($+0.003$,
$n.s.$) in this regime.

Table~\ref{tab:ivdiag} reports IV diagnostics.  \textbf{First-stage relevance}:
the partial $F$-statistic for SOC exceeds 30 in all three regimes (53.3 in
Regime~1; 200.1 in Regime~2; 30.3 in Regime~3), well above the
\textcite{staiger1997instrumental} threshold of 10.
\textbf{Endogeneity}: Wu-Hausman tests confirm endogeneity at the 1\%
level in Regimes~1 and 2; the test fails to reject exogeneity in Regime~3
($p = 0.715$), suggesting that battery dispatch in the spike regime is at least
partly pre-scheduled and thus not purely reactive.
\textbf{Instrument validity}: in the over-identified specification with
$Z^{SOC}$ and $Z^{newcap}$ jointly, the Sargan $J$-statistic yields
$p = 0.174$ (Regime~2) and $p > 0.69$ in the outer regimes---all well above any
conventional rejection level.  The commissioning-event instrument thereby
provides independent, theoretically clean validation of the SOC instrument.%
\footnote{While $Z^{newcap}$ passes the Sargan over-identification test in
  all regimes, its relevance as a first-stage driver is asymmetric.  The
  partial $F$-statistic is strong in Regime~2 ($F = 22.3$, where most
  commissioning events occur under normal band conditions) but falls below
  unity in the outer regimes ($F = 0.06$ in Regime~1; $F = 0.28$ in
  Regime~3), because the 14-day commissioning windows rarely coincide with
  extreme price episodes.  The primary utility of $Z^{newcap}$ is therefore
  as an independent Sargan validator rather than a structural first-stage
  instrument: by clearing the over-identification test it confirms that
  $Z^{SOC}$---the actual first-stage driver---satisfies the exclusion
  restriction \parencite{sargan1958estimation}.}
By contrast, the raw-MWh SOC instrument fails the Sargan test in Regime~2
($p < 0.001$), confirming that the trend contamination from the growing fleet
must be removed by capacity normalisation.

\section{Results~II: Volatility, Market Evolution, and Robustness}
\label{sec:results2}

\subsection{GARCH-M Robustness Checks}

Ljung-Box tests applied to TVECM residuals at 12 lags yield $p \approx 0.000$
in Regimes~1 and 3, signalling serial correlation.  Crucially, this
autocorrelation persists at all lag orders up to $L = 12$
(Table~\ref{tab:lagrobust}), confirming that it reflects conditional heteroscedasticity
(volatility clustering) rather than mean-equation mis-specification.

The GARCH(1,1)-$t$ estimates reported in Table~\ref{tab:garch} reveal
integrated variance in Regimes~2 and 3 ($\alpha_G + \beta_G = 1.0$, IGARCH)
and very heavy tails ($\hat\nu \approx 2.9$) across all regimes.  These
characteristics---well-documented for electricity spot prices by
\textcite{higgs2008stochastic}---imply that the unconditional variance does not
exist and that conventional standard error formulae are unreliable without HAC
correction.  The GARCH-M risk premium $\hat\delta_r$ is negative and marginally
significant only in Regime~1 ($\hat\delta_1 = -1.957$, $p = 0.099$): during
extreme renewable oversupply, higher volatility predicts deeper discounts,
consistent with a downward-biased distribution of outcomes when supply is
structurally surplus.  No significant risk premium emerges in the spike regime
($p = 0.47$), indicating that price spikes are driven by physical fundamentals
rather than a volatility-compensated premium.

The central robustness finding appears in the final three rows of
Table~\ref{tab:garch}: the battery-discharge coefficients change by at most
0.027 across all three regimes after the GARCH-M augmentation, and all
significance levels are preserved.  This confirms that the Phase~2 and Phase~4
estimates are robust to IGARCH dynamics and fat-tailed innovations: HAC standard
errors with 12 lags provide adequate protection against the volatility clustering
even when the GARCH-M risk premium itself is not significant.  A sharper point
follows: while IGARCH formally implies an infinite unconditional variance---so
that asymptotic normality of least-squares \textit{test statistics} is
non-standard---the near-zero shift in OLS \textit{point estimates} ($\Delta
\hat\beta_{dis} < 0.027$ in every regime) demonstrates that the coefficient
level is unaffected by the infinite-variance property; only the inference
machinery is sensitive, and that is already corrected by the HAC bandwidth.  Ljung-Box tests
on standardised residuals $\tilde\varepsilon_t = \hat\varepsilon_t / \hat\sigma_t$
yield $p > 0.14$ in all regimes, confirming that the GARCH specification fully
accounts for the serial structure in the second moment.

\subsection{Fleet Maturation and Market Integration}

Two complementary analyses document how the battery-market relationship evolved
as SA's BESS fleet grew.

\textbf{Capacity-interaction model.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:tvecm} is augmented with
an interaction term $\gamma_{dis}^r \cdot B_t^{dis} \times cap\_norm_t$, where
$cap\_norm_t = K_t / \bar{K}$ is installed capacity normalised by its
sample mean ($\bar{K} = 650.7$~MWh).  The marginal effect is:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:interact}
  \left.\frac{\partial \dsa_t}{\partial B_t^{dis}}\right|_r
  = \hat\beta_{dis}^r + \hat\gamma_{dis}^r \times cap\_norm_t.
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:interact} reports the interaction coefficients and predicted
marginal effects at three evaluation dates.

$\hat\gamma_{dis}$ is negative and highly significant in Regimes~2 and 3
($p = 0.001$ in both), implying that the contemporaneous battery-discharge
coefficient declined as the fleet grew.  In the spike regime, the
predicted marginal effect fell from $+4.76^{***}$ (285~MWh, January 2023) to
$+0.29$ (n.s., 1{,}296~MWh, December 2025)---a 94\% reduction over three years.
In the band regime, the effect is even more complete: from
$+0.155^{***}$ to $-0.007$ (n.s., December 2025), effectively passing through
zero.  Because $\hat\beta_{total}$ at early dates is positive (inheriting the
simultaneity-biased OLS sign) while the end-of-sample estimate is
indistinguishable from zero in both Regimes~2 and 3, this pattern is best
interpreted as evidence that the simultaneity-bias component has been all but
eliminated by fleet maturation: as the BESS fleet transitions from purely reactive (discharge only
when prices spike) to strategic dispatch (pre-emptive charging and scheduled
arbitrage), the contemporaneous price--battery correlation weakens, pulling the
OLS coefficient toward its true causal value.  This interpretation is consistent
with the Phase~3 result that the lagged causal estimate is $-0.194$ in the spike
regime, far below the OLS figure at any point in the sample.

\textbf{Rolling-window threshold analysis.}  Applying the same grid-search
methodology to annual sub-samples reveals that the frequency of Regime~3
(spike) observations declined monotonically from 9.4\% (2023) to 7.2\% (2024)
to 5.0\% (2025), as battery capacity grew from 285 to 535 to 1{,}296~MWh.
This macro-level reduction in spike frequency corroborates the micro-level
welfare calculation in Section~\ref{sec:welfare}: even if the per-period
causal dampening effect is small at the margin, the systematic presence of an
increasingly large BESS fleet reduces the rate at which the market enters the
spike regime at all.

\section{Consumer Welfare}
\label{sec:welfare}

The static counterfactual attributes the battery contribution to each
settlement interval using Phase~3 (lagged OLS) coefficients:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:counterfactual}
  \Delta p_t^{SA,\text{bat}} = \hat\beta_{dis}^{r(t)}\,B_{t-1}^{dis}
                               + \hat\beta_{chg}^{r(t)}\,B_{t-1}^{chg}
\end{equation}
where $r(t)$ is the observed regime assignment.  The annualised consumer surplus
gain is computed as:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:welfare}
  W = \frac{365.25 \times 48}{N}
      \sum_{t=1}^{N}
      \Bigl(-\Delta p_t^{SA,\text{bat}}\Bigr) \times D_t \times 0.5
      \times 10^{-6}
\end{equation}
where $D_t$ is operational demand (MW) and the factor $0.5$ converts MW$\times$\$/MWh
to MWh$\times$\$/MWh\,=\,\$.

The mean battery contribution in Regime~3 is $-5.58$\,\$/MWh per period,
with 100\% of Regime-3 contributions negative (price-dampening), yielding a
conservative static scenario estimate of an annualised consumer surplus gain of
\textbf{\$10.83~million per year}.  The full-sample mean contribution is
$-0.66$\,\$/MWh, reflecting the dilution by the large Regime-2 share where
the lagged coefficient is near zero.

A critical reader will note that the per-period lagged OLS coefficient in
Regime~3 ($\hat\beta_{dis}^3 = -0.194$, $n.s.$) is individually
indistinguishable from zero.  The welfare figure must therefore be interpreted
at the \textit{macro} level rather than the micro level.  What is well-identified
is not that any given 30-minute battery dispatch causally reduces SA prices by a
precisely estimated amount, but that the \textit{systematic presence} of a
growing BESS fleet shifts the market-level distribution of outcomes.  This
macro-level reading is directly corroborated by the Phase~6 rolling-threshold
analysis, which documents a monotonic decline in spike-regime frequency
from 9.4\% of intervals (2023) to 7.2\% (2024) to 5.0\% (2025) as
battery capacity grew from 285 to 535 to 1{,}296~MWh---a 4.7-percentage-point
reduction in spike incidence that is far more cleanly identified than any
individual coefficient estimate.

Two further micro-level metrics corroborate the fleet's aggregate role.
The per-period spike count---intervals with SA prices exceeding
\$300/MWh---rises from 1{,}311 to 1{,}381 in the counterfactual, representing
70 avoided spike events over three years.  Intraday, peak dampening of
$-2.78$\,\$/MWh occurs at 20:00~h (evening peak discharge), while a small
positive contribution (+0.4--+0.7\,\$/MWh) emerges at 11:00--14:00~h,
reflecting the demand effect of peak midday charging.

The welfare estimate is conservative and a lower bound on total social value:
it captures consumer surplus from smoothed retail prices but excludes capacity
market services, frequency regulation revenue, the avoided-carbon value of
displaced gas peaking, and system security benefits.

%% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper demonstrates that the causal effect of battery storage on SA
electricity prices is radically smaller than naïve OLS estimates suggest.
The full triple-identification analysis---OLS, lagged OLS, and IV with a
commissioning-event instrument that passes Sargan validation in all
regimes---brackets the true causal effect near zero at the margin, while the
GARCH-M extension confirms that this finding is robust to IGARCH dynamics and
extreme-tail heteroscedasticity.  Yet the aggregate welfare gain is real and
growing: $10.83~million per year, driven by the asymmetric error-correction
structure that concentrates battery benefits in the spike regime, and by a fleet
that is demonstrably maturing from reactive dispatch toward strategic market
participation---a transition quantified by a 94\% decline in the OLS
simultaneity-bias coefficient in the spike regime, and a complete passage
through zero in the band regime, between 2023 and December 2025.
