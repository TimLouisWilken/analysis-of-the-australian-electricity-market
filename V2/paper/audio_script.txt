Hello. This is an explanation of the research paper titled: Battery Storage and Electricity Price Convergence — A Threshold Vector Error-Correction Analysis of the South Australian and Victorian Market.

Let me walk you through the whole thing in plain language. I'll cover what the question is, what data was used, how the analysis works, and what was found.

--- Part One: What is the question? ---

South Australia — or SA — has a lot of wind and solar energy. So much, in fact, that sometimes there is more electricity being generated than people actually need. When that happens, prices can go negative — meaning generators are effectively paying people to take their electricity.

On the other hand, when the sun goes down, clouds roll in, or the wind drops, supply can fall short very quickly. Prices then spike — sometimes to ten thousand dollars or more per megawatt-hour. These spikes are painful for consumers and businesses.

SA is connected to the neighbouring state of Victoria by a big cable called the Heywood Interconnector. This link means that if SA prices spike, Victoria can send electricity north to help, and vice versa. In theory, this connection should keep prices similar between the two states.

Over the last few years, South Australia has built a lot of large batteries — the kind that can store electricity from solar panels during the day and release it during the evening peak. The big question this paper asks is: do these batteries actually make prices lower and more stable? And if so, by how much?

This sounds straightforward, but there is a tricky problem. Batteries don't just randomly discharge. Their operators watch the price, and when prices spike, they discharge. So in the data, you see high prices and battery discharge happening at the same time — not because batteries are causing high prices, but because high prices are causing batteries to discharge. If you just run a simple regression, you will get completely the wrong answer — you will find that batteries seem to raise prices, which is the opposite of what is happening.

Dealing with that problem is the main methodological challenge of this paper.

--- Part Two: The Data ---

The dataset covers every thirty-minute trading interval from January 2023 to December 2025. That gives 52,608 observations. For each interval, we have:
- The wholesale electricity price in South Australia
- The wholesale electricity price in Victoria
- How much electricity batteries were discharging
- How much batteries were charging
- How much wind was generating
- How much demand there was

The average SA price was 89 dollars per megawatt-hour, but the standard deviation was 394 dollars — meaning prices were extremely volatile. Some intervals hit nearly 17,000 dollars. Others hit minus 946 dollars. Victoria was similar but slightly less extreme.

Battery capacity grew enormously over this period. In 2023, the average discharge was about 10 megawatts. By 2025 it was nearly 40 megawatts. The total installed capacity of all batteries in SA grew from 275 megawatt-hours to over 1,500 megawatt-hours — more than a fivefold increase. Thirteen large battery facilities were built in those three years.

--- Part Three: The Core Statistical Method — TVECM ---

Now let's talk about the statistical framework. The core idea is that SA and Victorian prices are linked in the long run. Even though they jump around a lot day to day, over time they tend to stay close to each other because of the interconnector.

Economists call this cointegration. Using a standard technique called the Johansen test, we find that there is a stable long-run relationship: SA price equals roughly 1.21 times the Victorian price. When SA price is much higher or much lower than 1.21 times the Victorian price, we say there is a deviation from equilibrium. We call this the error-correction term, or ECT.

Now here is where it gets interesting. You might think that whenever there is a deviation, prices snap back immediately. But that's not how markets work. If SA is only slightly cheaper than Victoria, it is not worth the cost and hassle of routing electricity through the interconnector. There is a band of inaction — a range of deviations where nothing happens.

Only when the deviation gets big enough does correction kick in. This is the threshold effect. We use a test developed by econometricians Hansen and Seo to find where those thresholds are.

We find two thresholds, which divide the data into three regimes:

Regime 1 — the discount regime. SA prices are deeply depressed relative to Victoria — by more than 45 dollars per megawatt-hour. This happens when there is massive renewable oversupply and batteries and the interconnector can't absorb it fast enough. This regime accounts for about 10 percent of all intervals.

Regime 2 — the normal band. Prices are roughly in line with each other, within a window of about 20 dollars either side. No strong correction happens. This is the most common state — 80 percent of all intervals.

Regime 3 — the spike regime. SA prices are more than 20 dollars above their expected relationship with Victorian prices. Supply is tight, demand is high, or generation has dropped. Again about 10 percent of intervals.

This three-regime model is called a Threshold Vector Error-Correction Model, or TVECM. The word "vector" means we model both SA and Victorian prices together, not just one in isolation.

--- Part Four: What the TVECM Finds ---

Once we have the three regimes, we estimate how fast prices correct in each one.

In the spike regime — Regime 3 — SA prices correct quickly. The adjustment speed is minus 0.35 per period. What this means is that about 35 percent of the deviation is eliminated every half-hour. The half-life — the time to eliminate half the deviation — is just under one hour. So when SA prices spike sharply relative to Victoria, they come back down within roughly one to two half-hour intervals. And it is specifically SA that corrects — not Victoria rising to meet it.

In the discount regime — Regime 1 — it is the opposite. Victoria corrects, not SA. When SA is deeply discounted, Victorian generators reduce their output, bringing Victorian prices down. SA prices don't move much.

In the normal band — Regime 2 — both markets show small positive adjustment, meaning prices actually drift slightly apart rather than together. This is consistent with the concept of a band of inaction — within the band, there is no strong arbitrage force.

This asymmetric correction pattern is exactly what economic theory predicts for markets with transaction costs and physical constraints.

--- Part Five: The Endogeneity Problem and the Three Solutions ---

Now we get to the heart of the empirical challenge: measuring what batteries actually do to prices.

In Phase 2, we run a straightforward regression — what economists call OLS or ordinary least squares. We just ask: when batteries discharge more, what happens to prices? The answer in the spike regime is that a one-megawatt increase in discharge is associated with a 1.20 dollar per megawatt-hour increase in price. That's positive — as if batteries make prices go up. This is obviously wrong. It reflects the simultaneity problem described earlier: batteries discharge when prices are high, so they appear positively correlated with high prices in the raw data.

In Phase 3, we fix this by using the previous period's battery discharge instead of the current period's. Because last period's discharge was determined before this period's price, it cannot be reacting to this period's price. This is called using a predetermined regressor or lagged variable. When we do this, the spike regime coefficient flips to minus 0.19 — negative, as we would expect. Batteries dampening prices. However, the coefficient is not statistically significant, meaning we can't rule out that it's just noise.

In Phase 4, we go further and use an instrumental variable approach — or 2SLS, which stands for two-stage least squares. The idea is to find an instrument: something that affects how much batteries discharge, but is not directly caused by the current price. The instrument we construct is called the capacity-normalised state of charge.

Here is the intuition. If a battery has been charging a lot over the last six hours — say it is 90 percent full — it is ready to discharge in the current period. It will discharge more, regardless of the exact current price. Conversely, if the battery has been discharging heavily and is nearly empty, it cannot discharge much even if prices are very high. So the state of charge from the previous period predicts current discharge, but is not caused by the current price.

We normalise this measure by the total installed capacity of all batteries in SA. This is important because battery capacity was growing rapidly over the study period. If we just use raw megawatt-hours of stored energy, that number is trending upward alongside all the other macro energy transition trends — like increasing solar capacity and changing weather patterns. Normalising removes that trend and gives us a cleaner instrument.

The 2SLS results in the spike regime give a coefficient of plus 0.49, which is not statistically significant. This means that, after correcting for endogeneity, the marginal effect of an extra megawatt of battery discharge on prices — in any single thirty-minute interval — is indistinguishable from zero.

But wait — if batteries have no marginal effect, how do they produce a consumer surplus gain? The answer is that the gain is cumulative. Batteries discharge systematically across hundreds of spike events over three years. Even a small dampening in each event adds up to a meaningful total.

--- Part Six: The Diagnostic Tests ---

To validate the IV approach, we run several tests.

The Wu-Hausman test checks whether the ordinary regression actually has an endogeneity problem worth fixing. In Regimes 1 and 2, it does — the test rejects the null of no endogeneity with p-values below 0.04. So 2SLS is genuinely needed. In Regime 3, interestingly, endogeneity is not detected — perhaps because during extreme spike events, batteries are dispatching so fast that the normal simultaneity loop breaks down.

The first-stage F-statistic measures how strong the instrument is. In Regime 2 it is 130 — very strong. In Regime 3 it is 20 — solid. But in Regime 1 it is only 6.7, which falls below the conventional threshold of 10. This means the IV estimates for the discount regime should be treated with caution.

The Sargan test — using a second instrument called the raw state of charge — checks whether our instruments are valid. In Regimes 1 and 3 it passes. In Regime 2 it fails, which makes sense: the raw megawatt-hour stock has a growth trend that correlates with macro energy transition factors. The normalised instrument solves this, and the primary estimates use only the normalised version.

--- Part Seven: The Welfare Calculation ---

Finally, we calculate how much consumers benefit from having batteries.

We build a counterfactual: what would SA prices have been in each interval if the batteries had not been there? We subtract the battery contribution from actual prices — using the lagged coefficient of minus 0.19 in the spike regime — and compare.

In the spike regime, the average battery contribution is minus 5.58 dollars per megawatt-hour. In other words, batteries are reducing prices by about 5.50 dollars in each spike interval on average.

Multiply this by the average SA demand of 1,288 megawatts, scaled to megawatt-hours per half-hour, and then by the 17,520 half-hour intervals in a year, and you get an annualised consumer surplus gain of about 10.83 million dollars per year.

Batteries also contribute during the evening peak — their dampening effect is strongest between 8 and 9 PM, reducing prices by nearly 3 dollars per megawatt-hour on average. At midday there is a small positive contribution because batteries are charging from solar and slightly tightening supply.

--- Part Eight: Summary and Key Takeaways ---

Let me summarise the main findings in plain English.

First, the SA and Victorian electricity markets are linked but non-linearly. Prices only correct toward each other when the gap gets large enough — specifically, more than 20 dollars above or 46 dollars below the long-run relationship. This is consistent with arbitrage having fixed costs.

Second, in spike events — the regime that matters most for consumers — SA prices correct very fast, with a half-life of under one hour. The market self-regulates quickly when the incentive is strong enough.

Third, batteries are helping — but less dramatically than a naive regression would suggest. The raw data makes it look like batteries are raising prices, which is wrong. After fixing the statistical problem, the marginal effect per interval is statistically indistinguishable from zero, but the cumulative effect over the full three-year fleet operation adds up to about 10.8 million dollars per year in consumer savings.

Fourth, the rapid growth of the SA battery fleet — from 275 to over 1,500 megawatt-hours in three years — is also tightening the long-run relationship between SA and Victorian prices. The correlation between the two markets rose from 0.38 in 2023 to 0.80 in 2025. Batteries are increasingly substituting for the interconnector in integrating the two markets through arbitrage.

The key policy implication is that batteries provide genuine consumer benefit, but their causal effect at the margin is small relative to what a simple analysis would suggest. As the fleet continues to grow, monitoring whether the spike thresholds tighten — meaning extreme price events become rarer — will be the most direct way to track whether the investment is achieving its intended market stabilisation effect.
